What the slider sets

The slider’s number is a distance, in percent of the stage’s width. Photo edges of one kind, e.g. two tops, that are closer than half of that distance end on one line, between the places where their rectangles are drawn: at 4%, edges closer than 2% of the stage’s width end on one line. A photo on a line stays there until its rectangle is about the whole distance away. So the same sketch can end in two layouts, depending on where the photos were before.

The loss

The loss has two parts, and the chart draws both. The first part, in copper, is how far each photo’s four edges are from its rectangle’s. The second part, in indigo, is the misalignment penalty, which charges two photo edges of one kind for the gap between them while the gap is under half the slider’s distance. Each training step changes the weights a little to lower the loss. The loss alone leaves a photo a fraction of a pixel beside its line, so ordinary code, outside the network, also keeps one number for each pair of edges: the number grows while the pair’s gap stays open, and it is added to how hard training pulls the pair together. A frame’s steps are undone when they raise the loss with those numbers counted in.

Training

Each change of the sketch or the slider gets at least more steps, and training stops once no photo has moved for steps, or after steps. Training runs at steps a second where the device keeps up, and faster for a moment after a pause, e.g. after the tab was in the background. One step takes about on this device. The program is operations in layers, and the weights are its only input.
