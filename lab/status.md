## Runtime additions since September 14, 2026

On September 14, Cheng chose the recommended option for three runtime questions ([decisions 1 to 3](ui-exploration/guide/review-and-decisions.md#decisions-for-you)). Every existing circuit compiled to the same program with the same values, e.g. IndirectOS stayed at 5,253 operations in 192 layers until the changes below.

- **Index reads.** `circuit.indexRead` and `circuit.indexReadAffine` copy `candidates[floor(index)]` in 1 operation at any table size, for an index range strictly inside ±2^24. A gradient into the index throws unless the caller passes `'zero'` or `'backward-difference'`. The `recomputeDepths` option recomputes layers after gather lowering, and is off by default ([index reads](../docs/execution-model.md#index-reads), [compilation](../docs/execution-model.md#compilation)).
- **Comparisons.** `emitCompare` builds an exact bit of `>`, `>=`, `<`, `<=` or `===` from declared ranges: integer bits when both operands lie on grids declared with `assumeGrid`, e.g. 2 operations in 2 layers for `a >= b` on a 1/64 grid, and a sign cascade otherwise, e.g. 15 operations in 14 layers for `a >= b` within 0..16,384. `emitMinimumExact`, `emitMaximumExact` and `emitClampExact` select one operand with such a bit, and `emitFirstActive` counts these bits and index reads of bits as proven bits ([comparisons](../docs/execution-model.md#comparisons), [grids](../docs/execution-model.md#grids)).
- **Rounding points.** The builder records boolean atoms, `emitCompare`'s operations, and operations marked with `markRoundingPoint`, so a backend may reorder or fuse every other sum ([rounding points](../docs/execution-model.md#rounding-points)).
- **Springs and time.** host-timing.ts counts 4 ms spring steps and writes a 4-number step matrix per stiffness and damping pair, which `emitApplySpringMatrix` and `emitAnimatedObject` apply. `advanceFrame` steps springs to each event's timestamp before applying the event, snaps objects onto their destinations past 300 steps in one frame, and with `'events and settle checks'` writes state back only at events, settle checks and snaps ([springs and time](../docs/execution-model.md#springs-and-time)).

On September 15, 2026, IndirectOS and its Photos app moved to these additions. Windows and thumbnails are `emitAnimatedObject`s that share one spring matrix, the machine runs `advanceFrame` with `'events and settle checks'`, every destination change reaches the machine as a timestamped event, including viewport resizes, and Photos lays out at the window's destination size. The machine reads a click's viewport coordinates relative to Photos' content box at the window's destination, so during a maximize, `photoClick` selects the photo that ends up under the pointer, though until September 16 the page didn't deliver every such click (see below). Each window's content box takes the destination size at the event, while only the frame animates and clips the content, so Notes' text reflows once per event instead of at every in-between size. The window hit tests compare each edge distance with `emitCompare`, with pointer coordinates rounded to 1/64 px and window geometry declared on a 1/64 px grid, so each comparison is an integer bit, and they decided like the previous form on every press tested ([comparisons](../docs/execution-model.md#comparisons)). `emitFrontmost` stays, since at 4 windows its chain is smaller than an `emitFirstActive` tree. IndirectOS went from 5,253 to 3,654 live operations and from 192 to 124 layers, and standalone Photos from 2,358 to 1,759 operations and from 91 to 67 layers. In window-manager.test.ts's timestamped session, committed tables and drawn outputs at shared times are identical in every bit at 10, 30, 60 and 120 fps and through 500 ms stalls, where the pre-change IndirectOS in lab/ui-exploration/codegen differs from 120 fps by up to 357 px at 10 fps and 71 px at 60 fps.

On September 16, 2026, the Notes flow from [lab/ui-exploration/indirect-os-2](https://github.com/chenglou/indirect/tree/exploration-2026-09/lab/ui-exploration/indirect-os-2) moved into root IndirectOS, in notes.ts, notes-walker.ts and notes-paragraph.ts. Notes lays out its paragraph around the windows stacked above it, at their destinations, so words move only at events, and breaks lines where npm @chenglou/pretext's `layoutNextLineRange` does. The window controls, the dock items and Photos' content now act on release, like native buttons: a press arms the control under it, a release over the armed control acts whatever path the pointer took between them, and a release elsewhere or a pointercancel acts on nothing. This differs from indirect-os-2, where moving more than 4 px from the press and back cancels the press. The page no longer listens for `click` on Photos' drawn content: every release reaches Photos at its point, so during a maximize a release at a photo's destination opens that photo even where the drawn frame doesn't cover the point yet. Arrow keys and Escape reach Photos only while Photos is the focused window. IndirectOS went from 241 to 438 source cells, from 3,654 to 14,019 live operations, from 124 to 275 layers, from 7,890 to 31,359 stored weights, from 12 to 908 gather reads, and one evaluation from about 25 µs to about 81 µs. At 60 fps, an idle frame went from about 74 µs to about 97 µs, a hover frame with 4 pointer moves from about 353 µs with 5 evaluations to about 183 µs with 2, and a title bar drag frame with 4 moves from about 353 µs to about 445 µs, with 5 evaluations. ## Known issues in production code

- IndirectOS runs one evaluation per distinct event timestamp, and validates the table and the root values in JavaScript at each one. E.g. in Bun, a 60 fps frame of a title bar drag with 4 pointer moves runs 5 evaluations in about 403 µs, where one evaluation of the program alone takes about 76 µs. Since September 16, 2026, while nothing animates and no press is held, a frame whose events are all pointer moves runs only the last move, e.g. a hover frame with 4 moves runs 2 evaluations, the move and the frame, in about 164 µs, where running every move takes 5 evaluations. Moves during a drag, a resize or an animation each still run. These µs were measured after the [engineering guide pass](#engineering-guide-pass-september-17-2026) of September 17, 2026. Before its changes, the pass measured 450 µs for the drag frame, 83 µs for the evaluation and 187 µs for the hover frame, and the September 16 session above measured about 445, 81 and 183 µs. In Chrome, the JavaScript checks weren't small before that pass: V8 kept deoptimizing cells.ts's validator, which took about 129 µs of a real-page hover frame and 277 µs of a drag frame, and takes about 10 and 13 µs since commit 424a71a. The lab measured checks like these as small next to one evaluation: on September 13, 2026, [indirect-os-2](https://github.com/chenglou/indirect/tree/exploration-2026-09/lab/ui-exploration/indirect-os-2) measured a validator derived from the cell declarations at 0.75 µs for 759 cells, and in July 2026, [gather-lowering](https://github.com/chenglou/indirect/tree/exploration-2026-09/lab/gather-lowering) measured a check of every declared one-hot group at 0.2 to 0.7 µs for groups of 64 to 256 members, and about 4 µs at 4,096 members. Like [decision 4](ui-exploration/guide/review-and-decisions.md#4-what-should-agents-build-apps-from)'s first follow-up, gather-lowering and [index-read](https://github.com/chenglou/indirect/tree/exploration-2026-09/lab/ui-exploration/index-read) proposed checking stored cells when they're written back, instead of at every evaluation. [input](ui-exploration/input/README.md#rough-cpu-time-per-frame)'s window circuit ran a frame of about 5 events in 12.6 µs as one evaluation over a queue, against 16.9 µs with one evaluation per event.
- The title bar buttons are laid out twice: by the program's arithmetic for hit tests, e.g. the close button's left edge is `left + width - buttonGap - buttonSize` (window-manager.ts:478-483), and by CSS flexbox for drawing, inside the window's 1 px border (window-manager.css:98, 114 and 145-149). So each drawn button sits 1 px left of and 1 px below its hit rectangle. The hit rectangle extends half the button gap past the button on every side, which hides the 1 px offset. These sizes, e.g. the title bar height and the button gap, are also written three times: in the source table, as CSS custom property defaults (window-manager.css:24-29), and in a JavaScript copy at mount (window-manager.ts:1601-1609). lab/ui-exploration's [note on IndirectOS](https://github.com/chenglou/indirect/blob/exploration-2026-09/lab/ui-exploration/notes/indirect-os.md), section 2, found both before September 15, 2026, and both still hold.
- Range propagation doesn't track NaN: a ReLU of a wire with an open range gets the range 0..Infinity, and a later product can get a finite range while holding NaN. So when a source cell holds Infinity, gather lowering can skip an unselected product whose dense sum is NaN. `bun lab/ui-exploration/codegen/nan-range-case.ts` finds 10 source tables over 200 random circuits where the executor and the reference evaluator differ on today's numeric-ui-core.ts. The demos can't reach the case, since their JavaScript validation rejects non-finite sources ([codegen](ui-exploration/codegen/README.md)).
- Not production code, but agents copy it: most guide examples still use the older defaults, e.g. the toggle switch in [guide/sections/model.md](ui-exploration/guide/sections/model.md) uses rounding stages and `emitSpring`, which `emitCompare`, `emitAnimatedObject` and `advanceFrame` replaced as defaults on September 14.

## Reusable layout

Run `bun run start:reusable`. The demo compares ordinary wrapping, search for each layout, and the shared learned rule. Controls change viewport width, collection, item count, and captions. All methods keep reading order and required content; scrolling is allowed.

Under the authored preference objective, the shared rule transfers well to new counts and widths and leaves a visible gap with longer captions and unfamiliar crops; the loss table is in the experiment's [results](reusable-layout/README.md#results). This is learned responsive sizing within a fixed flow layout, not generation of arbitrary page structures. The wrapping baseline uses a fixed preferred width; we have not shown that the model beats a carefully tuned responsive formula or measured how much it improves on a width-only model. Every method has zero measured missing items, text overflow, horizontal overflow, and overlap in those sets. Resize sweeps still find abrupt reflow. The photo corpus is shared between training and evaluation; different crops are not new photographs.

## Correctness changes

- Whole multiplication and selection retain their meaning for differentiation while expanding to the existing forward atoms. Multiplication at zero now has the intended derivative in either operand order. Boolean surrogate gradients require an explicit option.
- Selection uses two masked products, avoiding overflow from subtracting large finite candidates.
- `roundAffine` preserves evaluation boundaries, constant and duplicate terms, and term order through inlining. Ordinary `affine` remains a simplifiable builder expression.
- Numeric bounds follow rounded FP32 operations and account for overflow. Min/max no longer claim ideal mathematical bounds where their arithmetic can drift. Their preserved rounding and weaker bounds cost some additional layers and live operations.
- The trace compiler propagates returns, rejects mutations in runtime conditional expressions it cannot safely handle, and compares nullable values correctly. Its source interpreter is independently checked against native JavaScript; intentional FP32 drift is reported separately.
- Real-source compiler tests use a verified historical source fixture. Compatibility with the latest external file is an optional `--live` check, not an assumption that another repository remains unchanged.
- Layout fitness includes direct parameter derivatives. The event demo retains queued and browser-coalesced pointer moves, including outward-and-back movement that cancels a click.

See [execution semantics](../docs/execution-model.md), [macro contracts](../docs/execution-model.md#macro-contracts), and the regression tests for exact domains. This is not a proof of all possible circuits or all JavaScript programs.

## Demos of traditional JavaScript tasks

Cheng asked for temporary demos of traditional JavaScript tasks, to see how the approach handles them. Each demo has a fixed capacity, named outputs for what doesn't fit ([behavior-choices.md](../docs/behavior-choices.md#a-fixed-maximum-with-a-named-fallback)), a plain JavaScript reference written from the task's definition, differential tests and a page. µs come from Bun 1.4.0 with each variant in its own process, and Chrome times from headless Chrome 153, on one machine shared with other agents' work.

- **JSON highlighter**, September 17, 2026 ([lab/json-highlighter](json-highlighter/README.md)): a text box of up to 512 code points, highlighted on every keystroke. The tokenizer (26 states) and the grammar (8 states) are state machines read from tables in cells, the container kind at the current depth is one integer with a bit per open container up to depth 16, the first error is a count of bits that flip once, and the bracket that matches the bracket at the caret comes from a running count of the positions whose depth passes the bracket's depth. Past 512 code points, `truncated` is 1 and the first error is `tooLong`, and an opening bracket at depth 16 is the error `tooDeep`. 4,575 source cells. The loop form is 15,928 live operations, 6,154 index reads, 1,043 layers, 28,744 stored weights and 155 µs per write and evaluation, and an empty text takes 147 µs, or 122 and 119 µs since the [engineering guide pass](#engineering-guide-pass-september-17-2026) later that day, which didn't measure the other µs of this item again. As prefix scans, Brent-Kung is 51,860 operations, 81 layers and 386 µs, and Sklansky 99,627 operations, 64 layers and 754 µs, so fewer layers cost more µs on today's executor. The plain JavaScript reference takes 11 to 13 µs. In headless Chrome, the loop form's evaluation median per keystroke is 0.4 to 0.6 ms. The program matches the reference on every output for 2,509 texts, and the reference is valid exactly when JSON.parse succeeds on the 2,404 of them whose first error isn't `tooDeep` or `tooLong`.
- **Regex search**, September 17, 2026 ([lab/regex-search](regex-search/README.md)): search as you type over 256 or 1,024 code points. JavaScript compiles a pattern, a subset of RegExp syntax, into a position automaton of at most S states, 16 classes and 32 class boundaries, or gives a named message when the pattern needs more. The program finds every non-overlapping match, leftmost, then longest, with a reverse pass and a forward pass, each one product of a transition matrix with a 0/1 state vector per code point. With the pattern as data in cells, one program serves every pattern within capacity: at S = 32 and 1,024 code points, 17,506 source cells, 2,297,760 live operations, 8,190 layers, 6,619,069 stored weights, 28,669 µs per evaluation in Bun and 30 ms in Chrome, 3.4 to 3.7 s to build, 3.6 to 4.3 s to compile, and 5.8 to 8.1 GB of RSS, and since the [engineering guide pass](#engineering-guide-pass-september-17-2026) later that day, which removed an array that the compiler allocated for every layer, 3.2 to 3.8 s to build, 1.3 to 1.5 s to compile and 4.8 to 5.8 GB; at S = 8 and 256 code points, 58,920 operations and 499 µs. With the pattern built into the program, at S = 32 and 1,024 code points, 133,529 live operations, 6,137 layers and 1,145 µs, and each pattern change rebuilds and recompiles the program in 459 ms in Bun, about 400 ms since that pass, and about 306 ms in Chrome, measured before the pass. The most expensive pattern found at S = 32 gives 253,704 operations, 3,618 µs and a 1,024 ms rebuild. The tests compare matches exactly with an automaton simulator and with a reference that tests every substring with RegExp, over 26 hand-written cases and 400 random patterns with 6 texts each. Later the same day, to test Cheng's idea of running a program's matrix multiplications as a few batched GEMMs, form (c) grouped the steps into a tree of matrix products, and WebGPU kernels in headless Chrome ran that tree one dispatch per level, 30 levels at 65,536 code points, for the reverse pass's bits, with 0 mismatched positions in every readback. On the CPU executor, the tree costs what its operations cost: 9,496,009 live operations and 94 ms at S = 32 and 256 code points, 15 times form (a). On the GPU, the tree is faster than form (a)'s program from 256 code points at S = 16 and 32, e.g. 1.84 against 14.88 ms at S = 32 and 1,024 code points, and at S = 8 about even at 256 and faster from 512. Against a plain JavaScript loop over the automaton, uploading N × (S + 1)² floats of leaves loses at every length up to 65,536. With the leaves gathered on the GPU from each code point's class, the tree is faster than the loop in Bun from 2,048 to 4,096 code points at S = 32, 8,192 to 16,384 at S = 16 and 16,384 to 65,536 at S = 8, depending on the run, and against the same loop inside the page, from 1,024 at S = 32, 4,096 at S = 16 and 8,192 to 16,384 at S = 8. Nothing beat a loop over 32-bit words, e.g. 867 µs against 17 ms on the GPU at S = 32 and 65,536 code points.
- **Text rows**, September 17, 2026 ([lab/ui-exploration/lists](ui-exploration/lists/README.md#text-rows-with-pretext-heights-september-17-2026)): a virtualized list of up to 10,000 chat messages in 64 DOM slots, whose heights come from pretext's line breaks at the container's runtime width, through 9 cells per row: the height at the row's minimum width, the minimum width and 7 width breakpoints. An appended or edited message only rewrites cells, `full` reports the capacity, and when a resize or an edit above the view changes heights, the row at the top of the view keeps its position on screen, also with a scroll in the same frame. At 10,000 rows: 100,004 source cells, 111,271 live operations, 122 layers, 202,135 stored weights, and 1,175 µs per scroll frame and 1,204 µs per resize frame through `createEvaluator`, of which checking the table before and the next values after take about 160 µs each, or about 1,030 µs per scroll frame and about 90 µs per check since the [engineering guide pass](#engineering-guide-pass-september-17-2026) later that day; at 1,000 rows, 12,235 operations and 129 µs, measured before the pass. At 10,000 rows, 1,290 of the operations compute the scroll position and the slots, and the rest compute every row's height and running total on every frame, so a scroll frame costs what a resize frame costs. JavaScript prepares 10,000 rows in about 490 ms: pretext's prepare 184 ms and the breakpoints 305 ms. In headless Chrome at 10,000 rows, the evaluation median is 1,435 to 1,485 µs, and all 10,683 checks of Chrome's text layout against the heights from pretext agreed. `buildTextRows` refuses more than 16,384 rows, since FP32 holds every quarter pixel only up to 2^22 px.
- **Many paragraphs on the GPU**, September 17, 2026 ([lab/ui-exploration/text-walker](ui-exploration/text-walker/README.md#many-paragraphs-on-the-gpu-september-17-2026)): Cheng asked whether pretext's layout, with lots of paragraphs, is faster on the GPU than on the CPU. One program per paragraph size breaks lines the way pretext's `layout()` does for plain text, at a width in a cell, with a fixed number of line slots and named outputs when a paragraph needs more. At 517 segments and 83 line slots, with integer one-hots, the walker Notes uses, it is 113,802 live operations in 684 layers, with index reads 27,634 in 518, and as the segment walker, which has no line slots, 8,244 in 4,646. On the CPU every formulation takes longer than pretext's 2.5 µs per long paragraph: 10.5 µs as generated JavaScript for the segment walker, and 198 to 683 µs on the executor. codegen's WGSL executor ran the segment walker over up to 100,000 paragraphs in one compute pass in headless Chrome, and all 102,764,544 checked line counts and 10,688,998,499 compared during timing matched pretext. When only the width changes and the cells stay on the GPU, the GPU takes less time than pretext in Bun from about 170 long or 800 short paragraphs, e.g. 3.88 against 287 ms at 100,000 long paragraphs. When the texts change, it takes less time from about 1,400 long or 8,700 short paragraphs, and only 1.7 to 1.8 times less at 100,000, since writing the cells in JavaScript and the upload take most of the time. One long paragraph takes about 200 times as long on the GPU, 0.39 ms against 2 µs. Line counts are exact for widths on the 1/64 px grid, and a width off that grid within float32 rounding of pretext's fit limit can give one more line.
- **Knuth-Plass line breaking**, September 17, 2026 ([lab/knuth-plass](knuth-plass/README.md)): Cheng noted that pretext has a Knuth-Plass demo, and that optimal line breaking is a shortest path over break points, which fits min-plus matrix products combined in a tree. A plain JavaScript reference and two programs pick the breaks with the smallest total cost for up to n break points and W boxes per line, with min(slack, 63)² per line in whole pixels and 0 for the last line, since the demo's own cost divides by a runtime space count. The chain form takes one step per break point, the tree form combines W × W min-plus matrices with Brent-Kung's network, and both follow the chosen pointers by doubling. Every value is an integer within 2^24, so costs, pointers and breaks equal the reference exactly on random paragraphs with ties, the reference equals every set of breaks on small paragraphs, and pretext's demo gives the reference's breaks on text where the two costs agree. At n = 1,024 and W = 32, the chain is 277,068 live operations in 10,232 layers and 2.9 ms per evaluation in Bun, the tree 2,823,382 in 230 layers and 22 ms, with 7.4 GB of RSS while building, and the JavaScript reference takes 75 µs. On the CPU executor, the tree takes 1.7 to 7.7 times the chain's time at every measured size. A page shows pretext's greedy breaks next to the program's, 4 evaluations per frame in 1.6 ms for the chain and 9 ms for the tree in headless Chrome, and an export writes leaf matrices and expected results. Later the same day, WebGPU kernels in headless Chrome ran the tree one level per dispatch, for up to 10,000 paragraphs at once. Every entry is a u32, so the GPU gives the reference's totals and pointers exactly for paragraphs of up to 270,532 break points, a check in Chrome matched the reference at that size, and 502,812 timing readbacks matched. With the positions or the widths uploaded, one paragraph on the GPU takes about 0.3 to 0.6 ms at every size up to 4,096 break points, longer than the reference's 1 to 303 µs, but less than the chain program from about 174 to 387 break points. A kernel over every entry, W³ sums per product, doesn't cross over at W = 16 and 32, e.g. 42.3 ms against 10.4 to 22.5 ms for the JavaScript loops at 1,000 paragraphs and W = 32. A kernel that multiplies only the rows that hold costs, with the paragraphs' positions uploaded and the line costs computed on the GPU, takes less time than both JavaScript loops in Bun from below 30 to about 190 paragraphs of 256 break points, e.g. 4.50 against 18.2 and 10.4 ms for 1,000 paragraphs at W = 32. The count depends on W, the loop and the Chrome session, since near 100 paragraphs one evaluation is mostly the round trip to the GPU, which changed about 2 times between sessions. Uploading line costs computed in JavaScript never beats the typed-array loop.

## Still open

The next interpreter question is whether fractional training examples identify the required addition grouping. Scaling to longer programs without a complete loss table, learning a search guide across task families, and discovering shared query/key functions remain untested. The data-adapter baseline supplies a small exact task and independent oracle for that next comparison.

For UI, the next evidence should come from broader content and an explicit human task, not from interpreting synthetic preference scores as usability. GPU execution, a general JavaScript compiler, learned arbitrary abstractions, and a full learned UI runtime are not delivered by these experiments.
